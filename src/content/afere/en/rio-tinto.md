title: Rio Tinto
---

The technology used to extract lithium from ores, such as jadarite, is in itself sufficient cause for concern. The widespread violations of the law by Rio Tinto and its subsidiary Rio Sava Exploration, the suspension of the project in 2021 followed by its resumption in 2024 through the reinstatement of the Spatial Plan, the lack of transparency in the project's implementation, the drafting of laws and regulations to legitimize a single foreign investment project in the public eye, as well as the complete failure of the competent institutions and ministries to perform their duties, have further and justifiably fueled public fear and distrust regarding the project's safe implementation.

Regulations on permissible concentrations of heavy metals in water and soil have been rendered meaningless by repeatedly increasing the permissible limits. Elevated levels of metals, such as boron, in soil and water inhibit plant growth and may cause organ damage or even death in humans. Exploratory drilling for the mine has already resulted in significant contamination of agricultural land, groundwater, and the Korenita and Jadar rivers.

Rio Tinto and certain government officials have presented the Jadar Project to the public as a key component of Serbia's "green transition." The destruction of natural terrain and degradation of fertile soil for the construction of the mine, the contamination of rivers, soil, groundwater, and the accumulation of toxic mining waste, the disposal of which has yet to be fully planned, hardly appear green. Extracting lithium from ore is an extremely resource- and energy-intensive process that may also lead to the accumulation of large quantities of waste. Why is this extraction method being prioritized in Serbia when conditions for mineralization are more favorable in countries such as Germany, France, Austria and Spain?

Lithium-ion batteries have a limited capacity that declines over time. Since the majority of Serbia’s electricity is generated by thermal power plants burning low-quality, low-rank coal, are lithium-ion batteries truly necessary for the green transition? Lithium-ion batteries merely conceal carbon dioxide emissions from consumers. In Serbia, the priority has not been the use of renewable energy sources or the implementation of other measures that would facilitate a green transition, but rather the opening of a mine operated by a foreign company solely to serve European interests. This became evident when the Jadar Project was included on the list of potential European strategic projects under the Critical Raw Materials Act. The initial stage of ore processing requires enormous quantities of concentrated acid. Freight railway lines are on the verge of becoming unusable, with frequent environmental disasters caused by derailments and spills from freight wagons. Transporting highly concentrated acid by rail would pose an additional ecological catastrophe beyond the mine itself.

The future use of lithium-ion batteries is itself increasingly being called into question.
