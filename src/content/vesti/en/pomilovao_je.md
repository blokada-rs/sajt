_Pardons as the former president's final move: 49 decisions that raise the question of institutional accountability._

While students and citizens endured violence, pressure, arrests and criminal prosecution for taking part in protests, **Aleksandar Vučić repeatedly used the power of pardon to halt proceedings against people charged with violence against students**, as well as individuals facing serious criminal proceedings for corruption and abuse of public office. Before leaving the presidency, he signed decisions pardoning **49 people**. After the students' election victory, these decisions have become part of the previous government's political and institutional legacy.

On **25 October 2026**, citizens gave the Student List a mandate to form a new government. This opened a new page in Serbia's political life, but also raised the question of responsibility for the state of the institutions the new government inherits.

One of the serious problems is the pardon decisions Aleksandar Vučić made during his presidency, and especially in his last days in office.

On **27 September 2026**, Aleksandar Vučić signed decisions pardoning 49 people. They include people charged with physical attacks on students and participants in the blockades, activists of various political organisations, former and current officials, and people facing proceedings for corruption, bribe-taking and abuse of office.

The Ministry of Justice published the full list only on **3 November**, more than a month after the decisions were made. Until then, the public had no information about the identity of everyone pardoned, nor about the specific reasons for and legal consequences of each individual decision.

This is not an isolated case. During 2025, Vučić had already used the power of pardon in proceedings concerning violence against female students, including cases in which the victims suffered serious bodily injuries. That is why it is important to look at the whole series of these decisions and at how a constitutional power has been used in recent years.

## From pardoning arrested students to pardoning their attackers

During 2025, the power of pardon was at the centre of relations between the President of the Republic and the student movement. Different decisions showed how varied the consequences of this institution can be.

On **29 January 2025**, Aleksandar Vučić pardoned **13 people** who had been detained during protests and blockades. The list included six students, a dean of a faculty, an assistant professor, four school headmasters and a school auxiliary worker.

These decisions were made after students had demanded pardons for those arrested and prosecuted during the blockades.

However, a few months later the same power was used in completely different circumstances.

### Pardon for the driver who hit a female student with her car in New Belgrade

On **24 January 2025**, during a student blockade in Jurija Gagarina Street in New Belgrade, a driver, M. S., drove her car into the gathered citizens and students.

She hit two people, including the student K. V., who landed on the bonnet of the car, then fell onto the road and suffered **life-threatening head injuries**.

Proceedings were initially opened against the driver on suspicion of attempted aggravated murder. An indictment was later filed, and the legal classification of the offence was subsequently reduced to a serious offence against public safety.

Aleksandar Vučić announced a pardon for the driver, after which a decision by the President of the Republic ended the proceedings **without a court establishing her criminal liability**.

### Pardon for four SNS activists over the attack on students in Novi Sad

The second case took place on the night of **27 to 28 January 2025** in Novi Sad.

A group of students was putting up material calling on citizens to join a protest and the blockade of the bridges when they were attacked by four activists of the Serbian Progressive Party.

In the attack, a student of the Academy of Arts had her **jaw broken** and had to seek medical help.

Proceedings were brought against **K. S., R. D., B. N. and D. N.** for inflicting serious bodily injury and violent behaviour. The trial was already under way, and the student had testified about the attack and its consequences for her.

On **2 July 2025**, Aleksandar Vučić decided to pardon all four defendants and released them from criminal prosecution. The proceedings ended **without a final judgment on their responsibility**.

These two cases show that the same institution was used both to pardon arrested protest participants and to halt proceedings against people suspected of serious violence against students.

## Forty-nine pardons before leaving office

On **27 September 2026**, while announcing his decision to step down as president, Aleksandar Vučić said he had pardoned a total of **49 people**.

He said that **23 pardons** concerned people allegedly connected with the protests and blockades. He divided them into two groups:

- **12 activists** of the Free Citizens' Movement and the STAV organisation;
- **11 people** he linked to incidents and violence against participants in the blockades.

Vučić said publicly that he had made the decision despite opposition from part of the commission and from various security services. This statement raises the question of the criteria on which the pardons were approved and **why the opinions of the institutions that took part in reviewing the cases were ignored**.

The full list was published only on **3 November**, when the Ministry of Justice announced that the decisions had been delivered to the competent courts and that only after that could the names be published.

The 49 people pardoned include people who faced proceedings for violence against students, but also people from proceedings involving corruption, money laundering, bribe-taking, abuse of office and the handling of classified military information.

The legal consequences of the pardons differ from case to case. Some people were released from further criminal prosecution, others were excused from serving their sentence, while in some cases rehabilitation or a suspended execution of the sentence was granted.

## People pardoned in proceedings over violence against students and participants in the blockades

The 49 people pardoned include **11 people** whom Vučić himself linked to incidents against participants in the blockades. For several of them, there are specific cases of violence, injuries and criminal proceedings that were ended or halted by presidential pardon.

### Marko Pavlović and the attack on student Sonja Ponjavić

Marko Pavlović was charged over the incident in Ruzveltova Street in Belgrade, when he hit student Sonja Ponjavić with his car while people were paying tribute to the victims of the canopy collapse.

He faced proceedings for attempted aggravated murder. The trial was under way, but no final determination of responsibility had been reached.

Vučić pardoned him and released him from further criminal prosecution.

### Gorski Matović and the attack on students outside the Serbian National Theatre

On **16 February 2026**, during a protest outside the Serbian National Theatre in Novi Sad, Gorski Matović attacked students together with a group of men.

Footage of the events shows him hitting a student in the head and pushing a female student, who then hit her head against a wall and fell.

He was indicted for violent behaviour at a public gathering. The prosecution sought a prison sentence of one year and eight months.

Yet before the court proceedings ended, Vučić pardoned him and released him from criminal prosecution.

Earlier cases of his violent behaviour, for which he had been convicted, are also publicly known.

### Miodrag Selaković and breaking through the blockade at the Faculty of Law

On **14 May 2026**, Miodrag Selaković drove his car through the blockade at the Faculty of Law in Belgrade.

According to participants' testimony and reports of the event, after getting out of the vehicle he also got into a physical fight with a student, and then hit 90-year-old Veroljub Sarić, who was taking part in the blockade, with his car.

He faced proceedings for a serious offence against public safety. Vučić pardoned him and released him from further criminal prosecution.

### The "Porsche crew" case

The people pardoned also include the four defendants in the case known as the "Porsche crew" case: Stevica Kiš, Aleksandar Kocić, Ognjen Stanković and Nemanja Narači.

The case concerns an incident on **13 December 2024** near the Futoška market in Novi Sad. During the "Zastani, Srbijo" ("Stop, Serbia") action, a Porsche drove through the gathered citizens, and the men in the group then came back and physically attacked those gathered.

The proceedings concerned violent behaviour, serious bodily injury and causing general danger.

They were charged with various criminal offences:

- Stevica Kiš and Aleksandar Kocić with violent behaviour at a public gathering;
- Ognjen Stanković with violent behaviour and inflicting serious bodily injury;
- Nemanja Narači with violent behaviour, inflicting serious bodily injury and causing general danger.

**All four were released from further criminal prosecution by the pardon decision.**

### Radoš Kadijević, Slobodan Pećinar and Željko Milivojević

These three faced proceedings over an attack on Miloš Petković, an activist from Ub.

According to the injured party's statements, the attack was connected with his political and activist work. In one of the proceedings, Slobodan Pećinar was convicted at first instance and given a suspended sentence for attacking Petković.

This case, too, ended with a pardon.

### Slobodan Đukić and the violence in Kosjerić

The list also includes Slobodan Đukić, who faced proceedings after a complaint by Zoran Jovanović, a resident of Kosjerić.

Jovanović claimed that Đukić had beaten him in the mayor's office after he had come to ask when the works on his street would be finished. He reported losing consciousness and suffering injuries, while the mayor disputed his version of events.

## Twelve activists of the Free Citizens' Movement and STAV

A separate group consists of **12 people** from the case concerning activists of the Free Citizens' Movement and the STAV organisation:

- Lazar Dinić
- Marija Vasić
- Lado Jovović
- Mladen Cvijetić
- Srđan Đurić
- Davor Stefanović
- Mila Pajić
- Branislav Đorđević
- Doroteja Antić
- Dejan Bagarić
- Jovan Dražić
- Anja Pitulić

They faced proceedings on suspicion of preparing an offence against the constitutional order and security of Serbia, in connection with alleged plans for a violent entry into state institutions during the protest of **15 March 2025**.

According to the indictment, a meeting held on 12 March 2025 at the premises of the Free Citizens' Movement discussed ways of encouraging violent action. The defence disputed the charges and the way the evidence had been obtained.

These 12 people are not part of the student movement. They are activists of separate political and civic organisations. The proceedings against them ended with a pardon, without a final judgment.

## Twenty-six pardons in other criminal proceedings

The remaining **26 people** on the list are officials, former holders of public office, doctors and others who faced proceedings for various criminal offences.

Those pardoned include **15 former members of the City of Niš Emergency Situations Headquarters**:

- Dragan Trajković

- Sanja Stojanović
- Dušica Davidović

They faced proceedings on suspicion of abuse of office in connection with decisions to hire the construction companies "Vodomonteri" and "Sim puk gradnja" during emergencies in Niš.

According to the prosecution, between March 2022 and July 2024, works were commissioned outside regular public procurement procedures, and the city budget was suspected of having been depleted by the amount of **843,336,450 dinars**.

The pardon released them from further criminal prosecution before a court could establish their responsibility for the handling of public money.

### Former officials and other criminal proceedings

**Dijana Hrkalović**, a former state secretary at the Ministry of Internal Affairs, was pardoned in an influence-peddling case. She had been sentenced at first instance to 16 months in prison, but the Court of Appeal overturned that judgment and ordered a retrial. The charges concerned the alleged use of her position at the Ministry to influence police investigations and the handling of evidence in certain cases.

**Dragana Živadinović**, a former president of the Higher Court in Niš, was arrested in 2023 on suspicion of accepting a hotel voucher in return for a decision on the early release of a convicted person. This case, too, is covered by the pardon.

**Nebojša Đokić** was pardoned in a money laundering case. In his case there is a particularly important discrepancy in the available court documents: the Court of Appeal published information about a final conviction in June 2026, while the reports on the pardon state that he was released from criminal prosecution. This discrepancy needs to be clarified on the basis of the individual decision.

**Boban Džunić and Nikola Božić** were pardoned in proceedings for abuse of office. Džunić is a former mayor of the Palilula municipality in Niš and was the subject of investigations into irregularities in projects funded by EU funds.

**Igor Elez**, a doctor from Novi Sad, faced proceedings on suspicion of taking money from patients to get them operations outside the official waiting list. According to published reports, he took a total of 4,200 euros from three patients.

**Bratislav Todorović**, a doctor from Niš, was given a final sentence in 2014 of imprisonment and a fine for bribe-taking. **He applied for a pardon on 8 October 2024.** The pardon granted him rehabilitation.

**Igor Novaković**, a former director of "Jugoistok" and a former president of the Niš City Assembly, was given a final sentence of one year and six months in prison for abuse of office. **He applied for a pardon on 11 June 2026.** The pardon provides that the sentence and the security measure will not be carried out, on condition that he does not commit a new criminal offence in the next five years.

**Miloš Dugalić** was pardoned on the basis of an application he **submitted on 20 August 2026**, which released him from serving the rest of the prison sentence imposed by a judgment of the Court of Appeal in Belgrade. The court's publicly available statement does not contain enough information about the offence and the circumstances of the proceedings for a more detailed account.

**Tomo Stojić** is also on the list, but the available information is not sufficient to establish his identity and the specific court case reliably.

**Milosav Simović**, a retired **lieutenant general** and former commander of the Serbian Army, was sentenced to six months of house arrest for removing military documents, including documents marked confidential and state secret. This happened when he left military service. His defence said that he had taken the documents home to sort them out during a short handover period, with no intention of endangering the Armed Forces or the state. After the final judgment, he applied for a pardon on 12 November 2025, and it was granted in September 2026.

## What does the power of pardon actually allow?

The right of pardon is a constitutional power of the President of the Republic, set out in Article **112 of the Constitution of Serbia**.

It allows a named individual to be granted:

- release from criminal prosecution;
- full or partial release from serving a sentence;
- commutation of a sentence to a lighter one or to a suspended sentence;
- rehabilitation;
- the removal or shortening of certain legal consequences of a conviction.

The existence of this institution is not in itself contested. There are circumstances in a legal system in which a pardon can serve a legitimate purpose, especially when it is necessary to remove the disproportionate consequences of a sentence or to take particular humanitarian and legal circumstances into account.

The problem arises when the power is used in a way that takes the place of a court's decision and prevents proceedings of public importance from ending with the facts being established.

Pardons in proceedings concerning physical attacks on students are particularly worrying, because they halt proceedings in which the injured parties should have the chance to testify in court, the evidence should be examined and responsibility established. Cases in which the pardons covered officials and others from proceedings involving corruption, money laundering, bribe-taking and the misuse of public funds are a further cause for concern.

Under the Law on Pardon, proceedings can be initiated **on application or ex officio**. For a pardon that releases a person from criminal prosecution, proceedings are initiated ex officio, on the initiative of the Minister of Justice. In the regular procedure, the opinion of the competent court also plays an important role.

That is why the questions of who initiated the proceedings, what opinions the competent authorities gave, and whether all steps were carried out in accordance with the law are of particular public importance.

## Serbia must belong to its citizens, not to individuals

The events of the past two years have shown how important it is for institutions to be independent of the political will of individuals. Students took to the streets, blockaded faculties and organised protests, demanding accountability for violence, corruption and institutional failures.

The canopy collapse in Novi Sad on **1 November 2024** began a wave of civic resistance that grew into a struggle for fundamental change in the state. That struggle was not about replacing some people with others, but about establishing a system in which **no one will be above the law.**

The election victory marked a change in the political circumstances, but it does not erase the consequences of decisions made in the previous period.

The forty-nine pardons from the last days of Vučić's term, together with the earlier decisions of 2025, remain an important indicator of how the previous government used the institutions. The public has the right to know whether the institutions acted lawfully, who made the decisions, and whether someone's political influence was put ahead of justice.

A state in which accountability ends with a change of government is not a state of equal citizens. Serbia must be a state in which violence, corruption and the abuse of public powers cannot be removed from public life with a single political signature.

**No one may be above the institutions, and the institutions must belong to the citizens.**
